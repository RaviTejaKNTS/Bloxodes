# Bloxodes Article Editorial Standard

Apply this standard to article research, writing, editing, and review. Specialized article skills retain their data, media, and output contracts. Beebom Roblox coverage is the editorial benchmark for search-aware headings, conversational explanations, and practical depth. Use the maintained study and examples to understand those choices, not to copy prose or force one layout onto every topic.

## Editorial freedom

These are quality criteria, not an article template. Choose the outline, heading hierarchy, opening approach, prose/table balance, and ending that serve this topic. Examples illustrate decisions rather than sentences to imitate. A reviewer should flag missing answers, unsupported claims, confusing organization, repetition, or an unnatural voice, and leave the writer room to solve them. Do not turn feedback from one article into mandatory sections, phrases, jokes, word counts, or a universal sequence for every format.

## Audience and voice

- Write for a US audience in American English. Address readers as "you" when useful. Sound like a knowledgeable gaming writer: clear, conversational, concrete, and comfortable explaining the game.
- Explain the topic as if a player asked you about it in chat. Use familiar words, contractions, direct answers, and natural follow-ups. Acknowledge a likely difficulty or question when it helps, then answer it immediately. Avoid a stream of detached feature descriptions or a question at the start of every paragraph.
- Keep paragraphs comfortable to read, but give explanations enough room. Two to four sentences often work; neither sentence count nor section length is a ceiling. Use a longer sentence when it connects a cause, condition, and result more clearly than several fragments. Split a paragraph when the idea changes, not to meet a quota.
- Humor is optional. Never require jokes, quips, personal anecdotes, or emotional reactions. Do not invent firsthand play, testing, results, or author experiences. Support recommendations with game details.
- Avoid hype, generic scene-setting, forced suspense, filler transitions, and repeated stock openings. Do not use em dashes. Natural transitions and brief orientation such as "Here's how it works" are fine.

## Reader promise and opening

- Define the reader's main question, starting knowledge, and desired outcome before outlining. Prioritize details that help them understand a change, complete an action, or make a choice.
- Use a title that names the exact subject and promises what the article delivers. Include the game name for game-specific articles. Fix misleading proposed titles instead of defending them in the body; preserve existing slugs on refreshes unless a route change is explicitly approved.
- Open with the actual change, goal, or difficulty, give the player a reason to care, and establish what the article will help them understand or do. A few connected sentences of useful context and a natural promise are welcome. Answer early without turning the introduction into a compressed changelog. Keep internal verification logs, timestamps, metadata checks, and research limitations in the brief.
- Key highlights, an "At a Glance" box, and a second quick-answer block are optional tools, not the default article opening. Usually let the introduction lead into the first useful section. Add a summary only if it serves a distinct scanning need, not because Beebom sometimes uses one.
- A summary may repeat facts developed later. Remove redundant explanations within the body and FAQs that merely replay it.
- Restating the title as “To unlock X, complete its questline” does not make a useful opening. Explain a supported reason to pursue the result or the concrete obstacle the guide resolves, then orient the reader. Describe a verified playstyle or benefit when relevant; do not invent one to make the introduction lively.

## Structure and headings

- Give each major subject one logical home. Order sections by player priority for updates, by prerequisites and actions for how-tos, and by diagnosis and useful fixes for troubleshooting.
- Write major headings in real search language: combine the exact game/topic entity with the feature, action, or question the section answers. Prefer "Blox Fruits Update 30 First Sea Rework", "Magnet Fruit Moveset in Blox Fruits", "How to Get [Item] in [Game]", or "[Game] [Item] Requirements and Rewards" over generic labels such as "Abilities", "New Features", or "First Sea Rework". A reader seeing a heading by itself should know which game/system and answer it refers to.
- Use the natural term for the intent: how to get, location, requirements, moveset, rewards, release date, level cap, best choices, or how it works. Only promise complete lists or exact acquisition details when the section supplies them. Do not prefix every heading with the full title mechanically. Repeating the game name where it makes a useful search phrase is welcome; avoiding all repetition must not strip the heading of context.
- Short H3s such as "Magnetic Power" are useful under an explicit H2 about Magnet in Blox Fruits. Major H2s need enough context to stand on their own. Keep capitalization consistent and omit trailing periods. There is no required heading word count.
- Use H2s for distinct subjects and H3s for genuine subdivisions. There is no heading-count cap. Choose descriptive feature headings for update coverage and action/question headings for procedures; do not turn every section into a question or clever sentence.
- Explain what a feature does, how it works, and its practical consequence when relevant. These are questions to answer, not mandatory paragraph slots. Do not bury a separate event, progression system, or reward mechanic in an unrelated section to save headings.
- Develop each subject far enough to answer the next useful question: how does it work, where do I go, what do I press, what changes if I hold the button, what do I receive, or what can go wrong? Include source-backed requirements, controls, rewards, and exceptions that answer those questions. Do not label useful mechanics "too detailed" merely to keep the article short.
- There is no article word target or brevity target. Match depth to the reader promise and available evidence. A dense update deserves several developed sections; a simple answer may be short. Remove filler rather than explanatory steps. An overview may omit exhaustive stats, but must still explain the central systems instead of vaguely saying they add power, progression, or rewards.
- Use tables for comparable facts, numbered steps for ordered actions, lists for parallel facts, and prose for explanation. Let prose prepare readers for a complex table and interpret a useful consequence afterward. Do not merely restate every row. A table should save comparison effort, not replace the reasoning that helps the reader use it. Include meaningful tap/hold differences, conditions, and outcomes rather than compressing distinct behavior into vague cells.
- A troubleshooting section must add a supported diagnostic clue, failure condition, or corrective action. Repeating the prerequisite list under several new headings is not troubleshooting. Integrate brief checks beside the relevant steps when they do not warrant a separate section.
- Prefer the player's action to process labels: explain who to speak to, what to collect, and what unlocks next instead of referring repeatedly to gates, sequences, or reward interactions. Qualify an uncertain number briefly when needed; explaining that a random drop is “not an official guarantee” adds no useful precision.
- Connect paragraphs where one idea enables the next: a prerequisite before a recipe, a meter before the move that consumes it, or a reward before advice on whether to pursue it. Natural transitions and brief recommendations are welcome. Avoid a forced transition before every list, unsupported combo claims, and stock reassurance.
- Give a substantial article a natural landing: a brief recommendation, next action, or answer to the player's remaining decision. Do not routinely stop on the final table row or interface bullet. A separate conclusion heading is usually unnecessary. A conversational closing question is optional when it invites a relevant contribution; never append one mechanically or repeat the introduction. Simple short answers can finish without a closing paragraph. FAQs are optional.

## Evidence and uncertainty

- Keep research confidence separate from public copy. Distinguish developer-announced features, observed live behavior, conflicting reports, and facts the research could not access.
- Establish preview or release status once near the top when relevant, then use consistent tense. Add a local qualification only for a distinct uncertainty affecting a decision. Do not repeat blanket "unverified" disclaimers in every section.
- "The developer has not announced this" requires evidence; inability to fetch a page only means research could not verify it. An unchanged game description or inaccessible event page alone does not establish whether an update is playable.
- Community authorship or the absence of developer documentation does not alone require a public disclaimer for each supported fact. Use narrow qualifications for real conflicts, approximations, or version limits that change the advice; retain the evidence assessment privately. Do not remove meaningful uncertainty or present weak evidence as fact for fluency.
- A how-to must explain each mandatory stage through the promised result. “Follow the current objectives” and “complete the questline” do not replace the actual tasks. A Logbook reference may help track explained steps or verified variability; it cannot conceal missing research. Early Access alone does not establish that particular steps change.
- Resolve central missing facts before writing. A narrower promise must be reflected in the public title, metadata, opening, and body and still meet the authorized task; a private scope note is insufficient. Do not replace essential answers with caveats or assume every detail on a competitor page is correct.
- Useful developer attribution and verified official links are allowed. Do not narrate source gathering, database checks, internal approvals, or research cutoffs in articles. Keep evidence URLs in sources metadata.
- Study credible competing articles for reader questions, organization, explanatory depth, and useful formats. Write independently from verified facts; do not copy their prose, distinctive flourishes, or unsupported claims.

## US dates and times

- Use written dates such as "September 5, 2026" and 12-hour times such as "2 p.m. ET".
- For verified releases or events, give Eastern Time first and Pacific Time when useful. Convert with date-aware America/New_York and America/Los_Angeles timezones; check daylight saving offsets and calendar-date changes. Never assume EST/PST apply year-round.
- Do not use the author, server, or agent timezone as the audience default. IST belongs only in a useful international schedule, not the primary US explanation.
- Omit unverified exact hours. Only include a public checked-at time for a live-status question where it materially helps, using ET and keeping it brief. Internal evidence timestamps may remain in the brief.

## Links and final review

- Link verified existing Bloxodes pages where they help with the mechanic, requirement, choice, or next action being discussed. Prefer linking suitable existing words. There is no minimum link count and no obligatory closing link block. Omit weak matches and invented URLs.
- Brief references to "this guide" or "this article" are permitted when useful for orientation, but should not replace an answer. Avoid catalog/database self-description in gaming prose.
- Review against the closest Beebom format example: does the introduction orient and help the player, do major headings identify the game and search intent, and do explanations anticipate the next useful question? Check table interpretation, natural transitions, supported advice, and the ending. Reject a mechanically correct outline with thin sections or a list-like voice.
- Check grouping, factual status, US localization, and natural flow. Cut forced humor, repeated caveats, decorative links, and redundant FAQs. Key highlights are never an acceptance requirement.
- JSON, media, route, and browser checks complement editorial review. Passing those checks does not establish that an article explains its subject well.
- Approved-brief article writing uses [one editorial revision before acceptance](editorial-review.md): specific feedback, revised copy, and a final read against the reader task. This replaces generic repeated polish passes. Preserve facts and media; do not turn prose revision into another research run. Focused factual-refresh jobs keep their existing scope and do not expand into whole-article rewrites.

## FAQ rendering contract

Article faq_json renders visibly after the body and supplies FAQ structured data. It is the sole home for optional FAQs; do not put an FAQ section in content_md. Keep only additional useful answers absent from the body, without a count quota. A complete body commonly needs faq_json: [].

## Concrete guidance from approved facts

When the brief supplies a useful named event, tool, condition, or option, explain that detail where it helps the reader act instead of replacing it with general advice about goals, routes, or relevant bonuses. For a “when to use” promise, supported timing modifiers can matter more than another caution. Do not force every researched detail into the article; distinguish useful omitted guidance from optional background. Check table rows and cells individually: rows must match their column category, and a “why it matters” column must explain an actual consequence rather than restate that an outcome may be useful. A short connected explanation is often clearer when there is little to compare.
